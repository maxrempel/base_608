Draft a public-facing blog post for MaxRempel.com in Max Rempel's first-person voice. Target 900-1300 words. The subject is a preliminary, anonymized Starseed blood-type genotype study and the practical opportunity for a stronger follow-up.

Required scientific facts:
- Five explicitly designated self-reported Starseed participants had consumer-genotype files. ABO was research-callable in four: three predicted A, one predicted O; one was unresolved. One A prediction had low confidence because the tag panel and causal deletion disagreed. Strict sensitivity: two A, one O, one low-confidence call excluded, one unresolved.
- No participant names or identifying family information.
- Consumer genotype arrays can often support preliminary ABO prediction but cannot reliably establish RhD positive/negative because direct RHD deletion/copy-number and complex RHD/RHCE structure are not adequately observed.
- The folklore to test is primarily enrichment of Rh-negative status, especially O-negative, not enrichment of A.
- Approximate White-American reference frequencies: all Rh-negative about 15-18%; O-negative about 8%. These are reference/donor approximations, vary by ancestry, and are not a substitute for matched controls.
- The current data therefore do not test the central folklore claim and establish no blood-type enrichment.

Methods and opportunity:
- A clinical ABO/Rh blood typing test directly answers the stated prevalence question and is preferable to buying whole-genome sequencing solely for this purpose.
- Existing good-quality 30x whole genomes can be analyzed with specialized methods that model RHD/RHCE read depth, structural variants, and homologous sequence. Cite RHtyper validation conceptually: 100% RHD and 98.2% RHCE accuracy in one 57-person validation cohort, while making clear that clinical serology remains confirmation and performance may differ by ancestry/rare alleles.
- New whole genomes are justified only if the project also wants broader genomic analyses. They are not required for a blood-type prevalence study.

Exact one-sample, predeclared, one-sided binomial planning against a fixed White-American baseline:
- Rh-negative baseline 18%: nominal P<0.05 requires at least 3/5, 5/10, 6/15, 8/20, 9/25, 10/30, 15/50.
- O-negative baseline 8%: nominal P<0.05 requires at least 3/5, 3/10, 4/15, 5/20, 5/25, 6/30, 8/50.
- These are significance thresholds, not recommended sample sizes, and assume unrelated, independently recruited participants and a hypothesis declared before looking at outcomes.
- Approximate 80% power for one-sample Rh-negative enrichment: if true prevalence is 50%, about 12 participants; 40%, about 27; 35%, about 44; 30%, about 80.
- Approximate 80% power for one-sample O-negative enrichment: if true prevalence is 40%, about 10; 30%, about 21; 25%, about 31; 20%, about 50.
- A stronger equal-size, directly measured control design needs more: approximate per-group sample sizes for 80% power at one-sided alpha .05 are Rh-negative 18% vs 50% about 27/group, vs 40% about 52/group, vs 35% about 83/group, vs 30% about 156/group. O-negative 8% vs 40% about 21/group, vs 30% about 39/group, vs 25% about 58/group, vs 20% about 103/group. Label these normal-approximation planning numbers, not guarantees.
- Recommend a realistic publishable first stage: at least 50 unrelated self-reported Starseed participants plus 50 ancestry-, sex-, age-, geography-, and recruitment-matched controls, all clinically ABO/Rh typed under the same protocol. If resources are very limited, a preregistered 25-participant pilot can be informative only if the enrichment is large, and should be framed as pilot evidence.

Mandatory bias section:
- Blood type may influence self-identification. People who already know they are Rh-negative or O-negative may encounter online claims, find them meaningful, and become more likely to call themselves Starseeds or join the study. This is selection/ascertainment bias and can create a real statistical association without showing that Starseed identity causes or reflects different biology.
- Recruit before revealing the blood-type hypothesis when possible; ask when and how participants learned both their blood type and Starseed concept; record prior exposure to Rh-negative/Starseed claims; use neutral recruitment language; include experiencer controls and spiritually interested non-Starseed controls; analyze people unaware of their blood type separately; distinguish association from causation.
